CoGeL: Constraint-to-Geometry Learning from Demonstrations and Failures for Safe Long-Horizon Robot Navigation

Jumman Hossain1, Nirmalya Roy1
1 Department of Information Systems, University of Maryland, Baltimore County, USA
10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026), Austin, TX

Abstract

Robots are often deployed in environments where safety must be learned from experience rather than specified in advance. Demonstrations, operator interventions, near misses, and failed rollouts can reveal which behaviors are risky, but they provide only local evidence. The key challenge is not only to identify risky behavior, but to make that knowledge shape the distance used for long-horizon planning. Existing goal-conditioned methods can compose short-horizon behaviors into longer plans, but they typically assume that the relevant cost structure is already known.

We introduce CoGeL, a constraint-to-geometry learning method that learns a soft safety signal from robot data and incorporates it directly into the goal-reaching distance used for planning. This makes the learned distance safety-aware rather than treating safety as a separate classifier, hard mask, or post-hoc filter. We show that errors in the learned safety signal induce bounded changes in the navigation geometry, that route consistency supports composition through intermediate waypoints, and that, under a calibration condition, the learned safety scores bound the expected number of safety violations. Experiments across wheeled and legged navigation tasks show that CoGeL reduces unsafe events while maintaining strong long-horizon goal-reaching performance.

From Local Safety Evidence to Planning Geometry

CoGeL learns a planning geometry from local safety evidence collected through demonstrations, exploratory rollouts, failures, and interventions. It learns a soft safety signal \(\hat{\chi}_\psi(s,a)\) and incorporates it into the one-step cost used to train a directed distance \(d_\theta(s,g)\) with Bellman regression and route-consistency regularization. The learned distance supports both local control and waypoint-based long-horizon planning.

1

Robot evidence

Collect safe demonstrations together with failures, interventions, near misses, and safety-monitor events.

2

Safety signal

Learn a soft violation score \(\hat{\chi}_\psi(s,a)\in[0,1]\) from the local evidence.

3

Directed distance

Shape the one-step cost with the learned safety signal and train a safety-aware directed distance.

4

Long-horizon planning

Use the learned distance as a directed waypoint cost so local behaviors can form longer routes.

Safety-aware cost

\[ \tilde c_\psi(s,a,s') = c(s,a,s') + \lambda\hat{\chi}_\psi(s,a). \]

Risky actions become more expensive before route composition. Unlike a hard mask, the safety term changes the planning cost smoothly with the learned risk estimate.

Route consistency

\[ d_\theta(s,g) \lesssim d_\theta(s,w) + d_\theta(w,g). \]

This encourages the learned distance to remain useful when a long route is decomposed through intermediate waypoints.

What the Analysis Shows

Safety error controls geometry error

If \(\|\hat\chi_\psi-\chi^\star\|_\infty \le \epsilon_\chi\), then

\[ \|d^\star_{\hat\chi} - d^\star_{\chi^\star}\|_\infty \le \frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma} \epsilon_\chi. \]

Waypoint error accumulates gradually

For a route \(w_0=s,\ldots,w_N=g\),

\[ d_\theta(s,g) \le \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} d_\theta(w_i,w_{i+1}) + (N-1)\epsilon_\triangle. \]

Calibration connects scores to violations

Under the paper's calibration condition, the expected number of safety violations is controlled by the accumulated predicted risk plus a calibration-error term. For \(\lambda>0\), the same result can be related to accumulated augmented cost.

Directional hazards require directed geometry

If the true directional cost gap is \(\Delta>0\), any symmetric metric incurs error at least \(\Delta/2\) in one of the two directions.

Long-horizon composition

CoGeL reaches 0.76 LH-8× success in wheeled navigation and 0.68 in legged navigation, exceeding the comparison methods in both settings.

Directional hazards

Replacing the directed distance with a symmetric metric reduces directional-hazard success from 0.84 to 0.54.

Limitations

CoGeL depends on the quality and coverage of the evidence used to learn the safety signal. Sparse or poorly timed labels can miss hazards or make the learned geometry overly conservative. The method does not guarantee zero violations, and calibration can degrade under distribution shift. Long-horizon performance also depends on waypoint coverage, so a route can fail if the graph misses a narrow safe corridor even when the local distance is accurate.
